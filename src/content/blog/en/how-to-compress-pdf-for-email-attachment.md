# How to Compress PDF Files for Email (Gmail, Outlook & Apple Mail Limits)

You finish preparing an important client proposal or job application, attach your PDF to an email, and hit send—only to see an error pop up:
> *"Attachment size exceeds the maximum limit."*

Email providers still impose surprisingly strict limits on attachment sizes. In this guide, you will learn the exact limits for major email services and how to compress your PDF files so they send immediately every time.

---

## Major Email Provider Attachment Limits

| Email Service | Maximum Attachment Limit | Base64 Encoding Overhead Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Gmail** | 25 MB | Safe file size is ~18 MB due to MIME encoding |
| **Microsoft Outlook / Office 365** | 20 MB | Safe file size is ~15 MB |
| **Apple Mail (iCloud)** | 20 MB | Supports Mail Drop up to 5 GB (requires recipient iCloud download) |
| **Yahoo Mail** | 25 MB | Safe file size is ~18 MB |

> **Pro Tip:** Due to email base64 MIME encoding, attached files grow by roughly **33% in transit**. That means a **20 MB PDF** can easily register as **26 MB** on email servers and bounce back! To be safe, aim to compress your PDF down to **under 15 MB**.

## Why PDF Files Get So Big in the First Place

Understanding why your PDF is bloated helps you prevent large file sizes in the future:

1. **Uncompressed High-DPI Photos**: Taking photos of documents with a smartphone often produces 12MP images (5MB to 10MB each). A 4-page scanned contract can easily reach 40MB if not compressed.
2. **Embedded Full Fonts**: Word processors often embed the entire character set of a font family (including symbols in unused languages) into the PDF structure.
3. **Print-Ready CMYK Profiles**: Documents exported for commercial printing include heavy color profiles and 300+ DPI resolutions that are completely unnecessary for on-screen reading.
